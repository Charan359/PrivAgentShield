\begin{abstract}
Autonomous multi-agent Large Language Model (LLM) architectures decompose enterprise objectives into cooperative execution graphs involving intermediate scratchpads, shared persistent memory, and dynamic tool calls. However, unconstrained internal communication channels introduce severe privacy risks, including confidential data leakage, privilege escalation, and tool-assisted exfiltration. While perimeter guardrails filter external user-facing endpoints, internal coordination channels can propagate sensitive records without triggering boundary defenses. To address this challenge, this paper presents PrivAgentShield, a policy-aware runtime mediation framework combining dynamic Information Flow Control (IFC) with Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC). We formalize the Leakage Risk Index ($LRI^*$), a non-compensatory minimax metric unifying payload sensitivity ($T^*_m$), lattice clearance dominance ($\Delta^*_{ij}$), and downstream egress reachability ($\Pi^*_j$) modeled via absorbing Markov chains. Deployed as an inline reverse proxy with a three-tier cascaded inspection engine (regular expressions with Shannon entropy, named-entity recognition, and transformer verification), PrivAgentShield dynamically executes ALLOW, SANITIZE (via session-consistent pseudonymization), and QUARANTINE actions without model retraining. We evaluate PrivAgentShield across a multi-agent benchmark of 31 labeled execution traces spanning ten attack scenarios, demonstrating a 65.0\% privacy leakage prevention rate, 74.2\% task completion preservation, and a mean runtime latency of 1.66\,ms.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Distributed multi-agent systems (MAS) coordinate specialized autonomous agents to execute complex organizational workflows through natural language messaging, shared memory stores, and external tool execution loops \cite{1}. While multi-agent collaboration improves problem-solving modularity, it expands the internal vulnerability surface. Conventional AI safety techniques focus primarily on perimeter moderation models (e.g., Llama Guard \cite{15}, Llama Guard 3 \cite{16}) and programmable dialog guardrails (e.g., NeMo Guardrails \cite{17}). These perimeter mechanisms inspect prompts at external ingestion or terminal synthesis, remaining blind to lateral, agent-to-agent exchanges.

Recent empirical studies highlight the Hidden Leakage phenomenon \cite{1}: while multi-agent deliberation may dilute user-visible terminal output leakage, intermediate channels---including inter-agent messages ($C_2$), tool arguments ($C_3$), and shared memory ($C_5$)---frequently transmit sensitive records. Internal data leakage is exacerbated by three structural vulnerabilities:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Summary Collapse in Handoffs:} Upstream agents summarize reasoning context for downstream collaborators, routinely stripping privacy qualifiers while preserving raw sensitive operational data \cite{4}.
    \item \textbf{Compositional Privacy Decay:} Non-sensitive context fragments exchanged across sequential turns can be aggregated downstream to reconstruct protected records.
    \item \textbf{Confused-Deputy Exfiltration:} Untrusted external inputs or prompt injections \cite{7} can compromise lower-clearance worker agents, inducing high-clearance coordinators into transmitting confidential records to unmonitored sinks \cite{2}.
\end{enumerate}

Privacy and AI governance frameworks emphasize appropriate handling and protection of sensitive information across data-processing workflows. Mitigating internal leakage requires inline reference monitoring capable of evaluating data sensitivity, recipient authorization, and downstream sink reachability.

To address this challenge, we present \textbf{PrivAgentShield}, a policy-aware runtime mediation framework for multi-agent LLM systems. The primary contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Topology-Aware Leakage Risk Index ($LRI^*$):} A non-compensatory minimax formulation that unifies payload sensitivity, lattice clearance dominance, and absorbing Markov sink reachability into an actionable risk metric.
    \item \textbf{Runtime Mediation Architecture:} An inline reference proxy integrating dynamic IFC and ABAC across internal agent communication channels.
    \item \textbf{Cascaded Inspection Pipeline:} A three-tier engine triaging messages across compiled automata with Shannon entropy, statistical named-entity recognition (NER), and transformer-based verification.
    \item \textbf{Session-Consistent Pseudonymization:} A context-preserving mechanism replacing sensitive entities with typed surrogate identifiers to maintain multi-turn coreference without exposing plaintext data.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Evaluation:} Experimental evaluation across 31 labeled multi-agent traces spanning ten attack scenarios, measuring leakage prevention, task completion rate, and runtime latency.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Output and Perimeter Guardrails}
Perimeter moderation frameworks such as Llama Guard \cite{15}, \cite{16} and NeMo Guardrails \cite{17} apply classifier-based or rule-guided boundaries to external prompts and terminal outputs. Although effective for single-turn moderation, they operate statelessly at system boundaries and lack visibility into intermediate inter-agent channels ($C_2, C_3, C_5$).

\subsection{Multi-Agent Vulnerability and Benchmarks}
Recent benchmarks have characterized internal privacy decay in multi-agent LLMs. El Yagoubi et al. introduced AgentLeak \cite{1}, auditing privacy leakage across seven internal communication channels. Mireshghallah et al. introduced ConfAIde \cite{6} to analyze contextual integrity violations in multi-agent reasoning. Security benchmarks such as AgentDojo \cite{2} and LeakAgent \cite{5} demonstrate that prompt injection and memory-extraction exploits can circumvent application logic. While diagnostic, these benchmarks do not provide an active, inline runtime defense mechanism.

\subsection{Information Flow Control and Policy Enforcement}
Information Flow Control (IFC) formally governs data propagation using security lattices \cite{10}, confidentiality models \cite{11}, and integrity policies \cite{12}, \cite{13}. Recent adaptations to LLMs include LLMbda \cite{8}, which formalizes dynamic IFC within an untyped lambda calculus, and AgentFlow \cite{3}, which enforces path-based flow policies via SMT constraint solvers. While AgentFlow focuses on formal invariant verification, PrivAgentShield provides quantitative runtime risk scoring ($LRI^*$) evaluated inline with low operational latency.

\subsection{PII Detection and Sanitization}
Entity detection tools such as Microsoft Presidio \cite{18}, GLiNER \cite{19}, and Hyperscan \cite{20} provide regular expression and transformer-based entity extraction. Standard sanitizers apply uniform redaction (e.g., masking with asterisks), which can disrupt LLM reasoning. PrivAgentShield integrates multi-tiered detection with session-consistent pseudonymization to maintain downstream task reasoning.

\section{System Model and Threat Model}
\subsection{System Model}
We model a multi-agent system as a directed execution graph $G = (V, E)$, where nodes $V = V_A \cup V_S$ are partitioned into:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transient Agent Nodes ($V_A$):} Autonomous LLM agents that process contextual prompts, deliberate, and exchange intermediate messages.
    \item \textbf{Absorbing Sink Nodes ($V_S$):} Boundary egress points, including database write interfaces, external third-party API tool executions ($C_3$), and persistent logging sinks.
\end{itemize}
Each agent $v_j \in V_A$ is associated with an authorization clearance vector $C_j = \langle c_{j,1}, c_{j,2}, \dots, c_{j,|\mathcal{C}|} \rangle$ defined over an information-flow security lattice $(\mathcal{L}, \sqsubseteq)$, representing access privileges across sensitive data categories $\mathcal{C}$.

\subsection{Threat Model}
We assume that underlying foundation model weights are fixed and untrusted with respect to privacy guarantees. An adversary may attempt unauthorized data exfiltration through:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Confused-Deputy Exploitation:} A low-clearance agent requests confidential records from a high-clearance peer, subsequently routing the output to an unmonitored external sink.
    \item \textbf{Contextual Handoff Stripping:} Intermediate context compression omits privacy metadata while retaining sensitive payload data across agent handoffs \cite{4}.
    \item \textbf{Indirect Prompt Injection:} Malicious external payloads compel compromised agents to transmit confidential variables across internal channels \cite{7}.
\end{enumerate}
We assume the PrivAgentShield proxy resides within a trusted computing base (TCB) where inter-agent communications are routed through the mediation gateway.

\subsection{Cascaded Inspection Engine}
To balance throughput with detection coverage, intercepted payloads are evaluated across three cascaded tiers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1 (Compiled Automata \& Shannon Entropy):} High-speed regular expressions identify structured identifiers (e.g., SSNs, credit card numbers, IBANs). Concurrently, token-level empirical Shannon entropy detects unstructured high-entropy credentials (e.g., API keys, private tokens).
    \item \textbf{Tier 2 (Statistical NER):} Lightweight statistical entity extractors identify unstructured domain entities, including individual names, corporate identifiers, and diagnostic terminology.
    \item \textbf{Tier 3 (Transformer Probes):} Selective transformer-based classifiers inspect ambiguous or flagged payloads for adversarial prompt injections.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Topology-Aware Leakage Risk Index ($LRI^*$)}
The core analytical component of PrivAgentShield is the Leakage Risk Index ($LRI^*$), a non-compensatory minimax metric unifying message taint, clearance dominance, and downstream egress reachability.

\subsubsection{Message Taint Metric ($T^*_m$)}
For a set of detected entities $\mathcal{E}_m = \{e_1, e_2, \dots, e_K\}$ in message $m$, payload taint $T^*_m \in [0, 1]$ is computed as:
\begin{equation}
T^*_m = \min \left( 1.0, \sum_{k=1}^K s^*(e_k) \cdot \omega(e_k) \right)
\end{equation}
where $s^*(e_k) \in (0, 1]$ represents design-defined severity weights assigned to data categories ($L_1 = 0.1$ for low-sensitivity public data, $L_2 = 0.3$ for internal operational data, $L_3 = 0.7$ for personal data, and $L_4 = 1.0$ for critical credentials). The entropy modulator $\omega(e_k)$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\omega(e_k) = \begin{cases} 
\dfrac{H(e_k)}{H_{\max}}, & \text{if } e_k \in \mathcal{S}_{\text{secret}} \\ 
1.0, & \text{if } e_k \in \mathcal{S}_{\text{struct}} 
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $H(e_k)$ is empirical Shannon entropy, $H_{\max}$ is maximum byte entropy, $\mathcal{S}_{\text{secret}}$ denotes unstructured credential candidates, and $\mathcal{S}_{\text{struct}}$ denotes structured identifiers.

\subsubsection{Lattice Clearance Dominance ($\Delta^*_{ij}$)}
Given payload sensitivity classification vector $R_m$ and recipient clearance vector $C_j$ across categories $c \in \mathcal{C}$, clearance dominance is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\Delta^*_{ij}(m) = \begin{cases} 
1.0, & \text{if } \exists c \in \mathcal{C} : R_{m,c} \not\sqsubseteq C_{j,c} \\ 
0.0, & \text{if } \forall c \in \mathcal{C} : R_{m,c} \sqsubseteq C_{j,c} 
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Equation~(3) establishes strict partial-order enforcement: an authorization deficit in any single category triggers an immediate clearance violation barrier ($\Delta^*_{ij} = 1.0$).

\subsubsection{Downstream Path Exposure ($\Pi^*_j$)}
We model communication reachability over execution graph $G$ using absorbing Markov chains \cite{14}. The transition matrix $P$ is structured as:
\begin{equation}
P = \begin{bmatrix} Q & R \\ \mathbf{0} & I \end{bmatrix}, \quad N = (I - Q)^{-1}
\end{equation}
where $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{|V_A| \times |V_A|}$ describes transient agent transitions, $R \in \mathbb{R}^{|V_A| \times |V_S|}$ represents transitions to absorbing sinks, and $N$ is the fundamental matrix. The absorption probability matrix $B = NR$ defines the likelihood of reaching an unmonitored external sink $s \in V_S$ starting from agent $v_j$:
\begin{equation}
\Pi^*_j = \max_{s \in V_S} \left[ (I - Q)^{-1} R \right]_{j,s}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Unified Minimax Risk Index ($LRI^*$)}
To prevent compensatory dilution---wherein low path exposure might erroneously mask an unauthorized access attempt---PrivAgentShield combines clearance and reachability via a minimax formulation:
\begin{equation}
LRI^*(v_i, v_j, m) = \max \left( \Delta^*_{ij}(m), \; T^*_m \cdot \Pi^*_j \right)
\end{equation}

\textit{Illustrative Topology Example:} Consider transmitting a sensitive record ($T^*_m = 1.0$) to an authorized internal agent ($\Delta^*_{ij} = 0.0$). If the recipient communicates only with internal summarizers ($\Pi^*_1 = 0.05$), the resulting risk is $LRI^* = 0.05$ (ALLOW). If the recipient dispatches tasks to an external webhook tool ($\Pi^*_2 = 0.85$), the resulting risk is $LRI^* = 0.85$ (QUARANTINE). Incorporating graph topology enables differentiating between contained internal flows and exposed egress pathways.

\subsection{Runtime Enforcement and Pseudonymization}
PrivAgentShield executes deterministic actions governed by operational thresholds $\tau_{\text{low}}$ and $\tau_{\text{high}}$:
\begin{equation}
\text{Action}(m) = \begin{cases} 
\text{ALLOW}(m), & LRI^* < \tau_{\text{low}} \\ 
\text{SANITIZE}(m, \mathcal{M}), & \tau_{\text{low}} \le LRI^* < \tau_{\text{high}} \\ 
\text{QUARANTINE}(v_i), & LRI^* \ge \tau_{\text{high}} 
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ALLOW:} The payload is forwarded unaltered across edge $e_{ij}$.
    \item \textbf{SANITIZE:} To avoid syntax disruption caused by destructive masking (e.g., replacement with asterisks), PrivAgentShield applies session-consistent pseudonymization. Detected entities are mapped to typed surrogate identifiers stored in an ephemeral session table $\mathcal{M}$:
    \begin{equation}
    e_k \mapsto \mathcal{M}[e_k] = \texttt{"[TYPENAME\_"} \parallel \texttt{UUID} \parallel \texttt{"]"}
    \end{equation}
    Surrogate mappings remain consistent across multi-turn handoffs in the same session, enabling downstream agents to maintain entity coreference without plaintext access.
    \item \textbf{QUARANTINE:} When $LRI^* \ge \tau_{\text{high}}$, message dispatch is blocked, the sender is notified, and an immutable audit event is logged.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
We evaluated PrivAgentShield on a multi-agent security benchmark comprising 31 labeled execution traces across ten distinct attack scenarios (S1--S10) and safe cooperative baselines. The experimental harness incorporates inter-agent messages ($C_2$), tool execution calls ($C_3$), and shared memory interactions ($C_5$) within its simulation framework. In accordance with the reporting scope of the evaluated traces, quantitative leakage metrics are reported for channels $C_2$ and $C_3$. Operational thresholds were configured at $\tau_{\text{low}} = 0.30$ and $\tau_{\text{high}} = 0.70$.

\subsection{Baseline Comparisons}

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\subsection{Discussion}
The experimental results demonstrate that runtime privacy mediation in multi-agent workflows requires balancing security enforcement against collaborative task completion. Perimeter guardrails remain insufficient because intermediate reasoning handoffs and tool arguments routinely bypass terminal filters. By coupling lattice clearance dominance with absorbing Markov reachability, PrivAgentShield differentiates contained internal communications from high-exposure egress pathways. Furthermore, session-consistent pseudonymization preserves syntactic structure, mitigating reasoning failures associated with standard destructive redaction.

\subsection{Limitations}
\textit{1) Detector Dependency:} Inspection coverage depends on underlying entity detectors. Novel credential structures or specialized domain terminology may result in undetected entities.

\textit{2) Transition Probability Estimation:} Reachability modeling via absorbing Markov chains assumes that agent interaction probabilities can be derived from workflow definitions or execution logs, which may vary in dynamic environments.

\textit{3) Benchmark Scale:} Evaluations were conducted on 31 labeled execution traces across ten attack scenarios; broader multi-domain benchmark evaluations and live multi-agent deployments represent essential future steps.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
This paper presented PrivAgentShield, a policy-aware runtime mediation framework for mitigating sensitive data leakage in multi-agent LLM systems. By unifying payload sensitivity, lattice clearance dominance, and downstream Markov reachability into the non-compensatory Leakage Risk Index ($LRI^*$), PrivAgentShield enforces deterministic ALLOW, SANITIZE, and QUARANTINE decisions across intermediate agent channels. Experimental evaluation on multi-agent execution traces demonstrated a 65.0\% privacy leakage prevention rate, 74.2\% task completion preservation, and a mean operational latency of 1.66\,ms.

Future research will focus on extending the framework to decentralized edge and federated multi-agent environments, incorporating formal verification for dynamically changing agent graphs, and evaluating larger-scale real-world deployments.
